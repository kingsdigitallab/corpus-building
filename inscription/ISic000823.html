Physical description

Support

Description
A large quadrangular base of grey limestone. The block is essentially intact, but with partial loss of the front upper right corner (including part of the upper right of the front face) and substantial loss of the rear upper left corner. The upper surface is additionally partially eroded in the rear right quarter. The right side has a vertical line of red paint from top to bottom some 2/3 of the way towards the rear, and the side of the block is covered with white paint from the red line to the rear edge (whether either red or white paint is ancient or modern is unknown). Although the upper surface is only partially preserved, the remains of two holes are clearly visible (presumably the footholes/fixing holes for a standing statue): a deep circular depression towards the left rear of the upper surface, 8cm in diameter and c.7.5 cm deep originally (59 cm from the front, 23 cm from the left edge, 16 cm from the rear); and a deep elliptical hole toward the front right of the upper surface, c.6.5 cm deep, 4.5-5 cm wide, and at least 15.5 cm long (the front part of the hole is lost; 48 cm from rear edge, 37 cm from right side).

Lettering

Very carefully and neatly carved, widely spaced letters, with a narrow simple v-cut and without serifs. Alpha with straight bar; beta with equal closed loops; epsilon with shorter middle bar; theta is small, circular, above the line, with a central dot (32 mm); kappa has nearly full length equal arms; nu is enitrely regular; omicron is small and above the line (25-27 mm); pi has a shorter right hasta, and the cross-bar does not overextend; rho has a large closed loop; sigma has slightly open outer strokes; omega is almost full size, nearly circular, open with horizontal feet.

Provenance

Place of origin
Syracusae medium certainty
Provenance found
The base appears to have been found in Acradina in 1734. Lupi 1734, the first published notice, merely refers to it as a 'basis nuper inventa Syracusis'. However, a fuller account of Lupi is provided in Schiavo 1756 (vol.1.5, p.17), stating that it was found in 1734 in the modern suburbs of Siracusa but within the ancient walls. This is in turn formulated as the part of Acradina close to the two ports of the city in the epigraphic 'Raccolta' of Cesare Gaetani (Syracusan antiquarian, 1718-1808, the manuscript compiled 1772-1784).

commentary

The reading of line 1 is much debated, whether it should be the substantive ἁγε[μόνος], or a participle, either ἁγε[μονεύοντος], or ἁγε[ομένου]. As John Ma, Statues and Cities (Oxford 2013) 20 n.27 notes, if the substantive is retained, this is a completely anomalous instance of an honorific dedication where the honorand is in the genitive (and, has been noted by Dittenberger the absence of a conjunction would also be odd); consequently, it seems much more likely, as Dittenberger already argued, that a genitive absolute should be understood in line 1, similar to standard formulations recording the presiding magistrate or similar. As Dittenberger also, rightly, noted, there is insufficient space on the stone for ἁγε[μονεύοντος] and so ἁγε[ομένου] seems much the most likely reading (the surface is sufficiently damaged across the end of line 1 as to make it impossible to judge whethere there was any sort of vacat at the end of the line; ἁγε[ομένου] would fill almost the whole available space (c.25.5 cm, against the preceding 55cm of text and 4.5 cm of initial vacat). The holes on the upper surface do however appear to be entirely compatible with a portrait statue (pace Dimartino), and for all that the formulation is untypical, the purpose must surely be an honorific for Hieron, given that he is the entire focus of the text.

The suggestion of Oikonomides based on an Epirot text, in Oikonomides, A.N. 1987. The Inscriptions of two dedications by Epirot royalty. In P. Cabanes (ed.), L’Illyrie méridionale et l’Épire dans l’Antiquité (Clermont-Ferrand), 121-4, at 123-4 to read ἁγέ[τορος] is critiqued by Marchetti, P. 1992. Témoignages épigraphiques concernant Pyrrhus. In T. Hackens et al. (eds), The Age of Pyrrhus (Archeologia Transatlantica XI) (Providence RI), 51-72, at 59-61.
