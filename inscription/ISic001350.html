commentary

The epitaph opens with the formula μνημεῖον followed by the genitive of the deceased's name (l. 1) and unfolds in a complex series of poetic images and gnomic sentences. In ll. 2-5 it is stated that endurance brings salvation, and that therefore those who aspire to eternal life must practice it. In ll. 6-9, the deceased defines himself, speaking in the first person (for which, see Agnello 1953, 79), as a sinner (l. 6 ὁ πάμπαν ἁμαρτωλός): this rarely occurs in Christian epitaphs, but other examples can be found (ICUR IV 10535, VI 16073, cf. Dresken-Weiland 2006, 308). Boniface desired to flee from this life and obtained what he desired through the intervention of justice (ll. 8-9 ἐπὶ τὴν δικαιωσύνην μεταβολήν): the concept of iustitia is rare in Christian epitaphs. In ll. 9-11 there is a final prayer for the granting of rest at the end of a senseless life (ll. 9-10 τοῦ ἀνεσθήτου βίου: the adjective ἀναίσθητος (sensless, which does not belong to the realm of the senses) is connected to death in epitaphs — cf. ἀναισθήτῳ περὶ τύμβῳ in IG VII 2543 l. 2 — whereas here it refers to earthly life, from which a respite is requested) for Boniface himself, for his wife (referred to in ll. 10-11 as "fellow servant in Christ", on the expression δοῦλος ἐν Χριστῷ, see IG XIV 442, 629), and for his mother.
